\section{A Learned Scheduling Policy: PPO vs. MPC}
\label{sec:rl}

Every scheduler in \S\ref{sec:utility-results} solves an optimization
problem from scratch at every re-plan. This section evaluates the
alternative: train a policy offline so that, at run time, it only has to
evaluate a small neural network rather than solve a fresh MILP.

\todoconfirm{These results are on \textbf{legacy utility scale}
(\S\ref{sec:formulation}), not unified — the trained policy
(\texttt{ppo\_oec.zip}) optimized the old single-factor reward and has not
been retrained under \texttt{--utility unified}. Do not compare the
utility numbers in this section against Tables~\ref{tab:scheduler-comparison}--\ref{tab:utility-components},
which are on the unified scale; retraining under the unified reward is
future work.}

\subsection{Setup}

The environment (\texttt{oec\_sim/rl\_env.py}) is a Gymnasium environment
with a \texttt{Discrete(6)} action space — commit one of 4 depths, defer,
or drop a task — decided per active task rather than per slot, so the
action space size does not depend on how many tasks are active or how
large the constellation is. The reward matches the MILP objective plus a
potential-based backlog-shaping term. The policy was trained with PPO
(Proximal Policy Optimization) for 300k timesteps across load regimes
1.5--2.5\,Mbps and 10 seeds ($\sim$4 minutes on a CPU), then evaluated
both in-distribution and out-of-distribution (a load regime never seen in
training).

Three findings, two anticipated and one not:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{PPO loses to MPC on utility, as expected} — $5$--$8\%$ below
  MPC in every regime tested. Solving the optimization problem freshly at
  every decision still finds a better plan than pattern-matching against
  training experience.
\item \textbf{PPO is $\sim 250$--$400\times$ faster per decision}
  (microseconds vs.\ hundreds of milliseconds) — the anticipated
  inference-cost advantage of a learned policy holds up strongly, and is
  the practical reason a learned policy might still be attractive on
  compute- and power-constrained flight hardware despite the utility gap.
\item \textbf{Unexpected: the policy's actions are provably rate-independent.}
  Its observation vector includes task properties, active-task count, and
  mean freshness, but no capacity or congestion feature — so its decisions
  are bit-for-bit identical between the 2.0\,Mbps and 4.0\,Mbps regimes at
  a fixed seed (visible directly in Table~\ref{tab:rl-vs-mpc}). It still
  delivers 100\% of images even at the most constrained regime tested
  (0.75\,Mbps, where \texttt{greedy-fixed-8} collapses to a fraction of its
  unconstrained utility) purely because it happened to learn conservative
  depth choices, not because it senses congestion. This is
  \emph{accidental} robustness, not adaptive robustness — the clear next
  step is adding a residual-bandwidth/congestion feature to the
  observation, which the original environment design called for but the
  shipped version omitted to keep the observation vector smaller.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Two comparison-fairness issues in the RL evaluation path were
identified and fixed before these numbers were produced}: the environment
had recorded RL deliveries with no propagation delay while every other
scheduler charged the real route delay (effect measured at $0.0017\%$
utility — negligible relative to the 30-second slot and 300-second
freshness constant, a correctness fix rather than a result change), and
the RL scheduler's own utility accumulation bypassed the shared
\texttt{utility.run\_utility} path and would have silently omitted the
fairness term under \texttt{--utility unified}. Both now go through the
same accounting as every other scheduler in this paper.
