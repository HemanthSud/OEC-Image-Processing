\title{\textbf{Downlink-Constrained Task Scheduling for Orbital Edge Computing:\\
Grounding Utility in Measured Downstream Task Performance}}
\author{Hemanth Sudhaharan \\ NICE Lab, North Carolina State University}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Low-Earth-orbit (LEO) satellites capture far more imagery than their
downlink budget can deliver in time to be useful, forcing an on-board
choice of \emph{what} to send, \emph{how much} to compress it, and
\emph{when}. This paper studies that choice for Orbital Edge Computing
(OEC): a residual-quantized VAE (RQ-VAE) compresses each image at one of
several selectable depths, and a rolling-horizon Model Predictive Control
(MPC) scheduler decides depth, timing, and routing under time-varying
satellite/ground-station connectivity. Our central finding is that the
choice of \emph{quality signal} used to reward a scheduler determines
whether MPC's benefit is even visible: under a pixel-fidelity proxy
(LPIPS), MPC beats the best fixed-depth baseline by only $1.2\%$; once
quality is grounded in the downstream task the imagery actually serves
(measured semantic-segmentation mIoU), the same comparison shows a
$32.2\%$ margin — roughly $26\times$ larger — because pixel fidelity is
nearly flat across compression depths while task-relevant quality is not
($+12\%$ vs.\ $+247\%$ across the same depth range). Building on this, we
present a single unified utility function reconciling five previously
disagreeing scoring definitions, evaluate ten schedulers (fixed-depth and
adaptive greedy baselines, flat MPC, congestion-aware routing, and two
hierarchical/two-level MPC couplings) against an offline optimality bound,
and analyze the resulting depth-selection decisions and sensitivity to
link-rate scarcity. We find that admission control — not routing
sophistication — is what separates the strongest and weakest MPC variants,
and that freezing a concrete route for longer than a few minutes does not
survive a LEO constellation's short ground-contact windows. A reinforcement-learning
(PPO) baseline is also evaluated: it trades a consistent 5--8\% utility
gap for a 250--400$\times$ reduction in per-decision compute time.
\end{abstract}
